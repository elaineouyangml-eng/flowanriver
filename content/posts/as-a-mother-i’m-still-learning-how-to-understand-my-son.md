I used to think I would be the kind of mother who rarely lost her temper. I imagined myself as calm, patient, and good at communicating with my child. I would listen carefully, explain things gently when he was upset, and manage my own emotions before they ever reached the point of anger. I had read so much about parenting that I genuinely believed that, if nothing else, I would at least be good at talking things through.

Reality, of course, has been far less gentle than I imagined.

My son has an almost remarkable ability to find the exact edge of my patience, and somehow, little by little, break through the calmness I once thought I would always have. Most of the time, it is not even because something serious has happened. It is the same reminder repeated over and over, the same habit corrected again and again, the same emotions appearing for what feels like the hundredth time. When all those small moments accumulate, I start to understand that patience is not an unlimited resource, and that the gentleness I once assumed would always be there can slowly wear away through the repetition of everyday life.

Whenever I come across another parenting article or video, I still try to adjust myself. I remind myself not to rush, not to shout, to listen before reacting. I tell myself that he is still a child and does not yet have the emotional regulation of an adult. I keep trying different ways of speaking to him, hoping that perhaps this time I will handle things a little better.

And yet, through his endless little episodes of “testing the limits,” I still find myself moving back and forth along that invisible line between patience and losing it.

Now that he is seven, he seems to have entered his first very obvious stage of rebellion. He has more opinions of his own. He disagrees. He questions. He resists. He gets frustrated over things that, from an adult’s perspective, can seem incredibly small. In the past, when his emotions exploded, mine would often follow. The more upset he became, the more impatient I became, and something that had originally belonged to his emotional world quickly turned into a battle between the two of us.

These days, I have started learning to step away.

By “step away,” I do not mean ignore the problem or let him do whatever he wants. I mean that when I notice my own emotions rising, I remove myself from the situation long enough to calm down. Once I am no longer being pulled along by his emotions, and once he has settled a little too, we come back to the issue itself. Then we can talk about what was wrong, why something was not acceptable, and what needs to happen next.

One thing I have slowly learned is that not every problem has to be solved at the exact moment emotions are at their highest. When a child is in the middle of a meltdown and a mother is already at the edge of her own patience, what we call “communication” can very easily become two overwhelmed people trying to prove that they are the one who is right.

Sometimes stopping first is more useful than continuing to explain.

There was also a time when I watched other parents share stories about the chaos of helping their children with homework and thought, surely that would never happen to me. I genuinely believed my little “angel” would somehow be different. Homework could not possibly become that dramatic.

Reality proved me wrong once again.

Now I move through homework, reminders, repeated instructions, distractions, frustration, and emotional outbursts almost every day. One minute, I may be trying not to lose my patience over a badly written character, a forgotten instruction, or some tiny thing he has done for the fifth time. Then the homework is finally finished, the books are closed, and suddenly the two of us are back to normal as if nothing ever happened.

There is a Chinese saying that describes it perfectly: during homework, the whole house feels like a battlefield; once homework is over, mother and child are loving again.

I used to laugh when I heard people say things like that. Now that I am living it, I have to admit it is painfully accurate.

It is also through these ordinary, repetitive moments that I have become increasingly aware of how different it can feel, as a woman, to raise a boy who is slowly growing into himself. We do not always express emotions in the same way. We do not always process conflict in the same way. We do not always respond to frustration in the same way.

At the same time, I also know it would be unfair to explain everything with a simple sentence like, “He is a boy and I am a woman.”

Because I have also had to admit something much harder: children are often mirrors of their parents.

Sometimes the things we believe are only occasional weaknesses in ourselves are things our children notice, absorb, and eventually repeat.

There was a period when, after being pushed to the very edge of my patience, I would sometimes throw things out of frustration. I knew it was not a good way to handle anger, but when emotions are already overflowing, it is very easy to release them first and reflect later.

Then this year, I began to see a familiar version of that behavior in my son.

When he was angry or emotionally overwhelmed, he also started throwing things.

At first, my instinct was simply to stop him. I would tell him that throwing things was not acceptable and that anger did not give him the right to behave that way. But later, when I had time to think by myself, something uncomfortable became very clear to me: I was forbidding him from doing exactly what he had once seen me do.

That realization hurt.

A child does not forget what he has witnessed simply because you later tell him, “That is wrong.” He hears your words, but he also remembers your behavior, and often what we do speaks much louder than what we tell them to do.

So instead of only asking him to change, I apologized first.

I told him that when I had thrown things in anger before, my behavior had been wrong. I told him I should not have dealt with my emotions that way, and that from now on, I would work on not doing it again. I also told him that I hoped the two of us could practice this together, learning how to calm ourselves down when we are angry instead of throwing things to release our frustration.

That moment made something very clear to me: parenting is not always about standing in front of a child and teaching him how to behave. Sometimes it is about seeing ourselves reflected in each other and making corrections together.

I still tell him what is acceptable and what is not. I still believe in rules, consequences, and boundaries. But I increasingly believe that if I want him to learn emotional regulation, then he also needs to see what it looks like when an adult faces her own mistakes, admits them, and chooses to do differently next time.

People often say that children are mirrors of their parents. It can sound like one of those parenting phrases that gets repeated too often, but when you actually experience it, it is not particularly comforting.

Because sometimes that mirror does not only show you your child’s problems.

It shows you your own.

Another thing I have noticed in our daily life is how completely absorbed he can become when he is focused on something.

If he is building something, reading, drawing, or deeply involved in whatever he is doing, I can call his name from nearby and ask him to help me with something simple, and very often the response is nothing at all.

No answer.

I used to interpret that “not hearing me” as ignoring me. I would think, I have called you several times, so why are you refusing to answer? But as I have learned more about some of the differences in how children grow, process information, and shift attention, I have started to see those moments differently. Many times, he is not deliberately ignoring me and he is not refusing to help. He is simply still deeply immersed in what he is doing and has not yet shifted his attention somewhere else.

So I have changed the way I communicate with him.

If he is deeply focused, I try not to keep shouting from another room. Instead, I walk over, make sure I actually have his attention, and then tell him what I need. Sometimes all it takes is waiting a few extra seconds, and a situation that might once have turned into “Why are you never listening to me?” disappears completely.

I find this surprisingly important, because adults are very quick to interpret a child’s delayed response as an attitude problem. Sometimes, however, the issue is simply that his attention has not finished shifting yet.

Especially in my relationship with my son, I am learning not to immediately interpret “no response” as disrespect, and not to label every frustrating behavior as intentional. A lot of conflict does not begin because someone has truly done something wrong. It begins because we interpret another person’s behavior through our own way of thinking.

I used to believe communication was mostly about explaining things clearly. If I was patient enough, if I explained things carefully enough, then surely he would understand.

Now I know it is not that simple.

A seven-year-old does not always express himself according to adult logic. He may not know how to describe why he feels hurt. He may not even know why he suddenly became angry. Sometimes what comes out of his mouth is “I don’t know,” while what shows up in his behavior is irritation, avoidance, whining, refusal, or a sudden emotional explosion.

If I only focus on the behavior, it is very easy to interpret everything as “disobedience,” “bad attitude,” or “doing it on purpose.”

But when I step back slightly, I have started asking a different question:

What is he actually trying to express right now?

Of course, understanding his emotions does not mean everything can be excused with, “He is still a child.”

Understanding his feelings and asking him to take responsibility can exist at the same time.

I can understand why he is angry without accepting hurtful behavior. I can understand why he is sad while still telling him that the problem needs to be dealt with. I can allow him to cry or give him time before talking, and still return to what happened once he is calm.

I hope he grows into a gentle person, but gentleness does not mean having no boundaries. I hope he is kind, but kindness does not mean constantly giving in. I hope he can express vulnerability and still take responsibility for his choices.

And because he is a boy, I also find myself questioning some of the expectations I may unconsciously place on him simply because of his gender.

I want him to be brave. I want him to become more independent. Sometimes I wish he would stop crying over small things, stop whining, stop reacting so strongly when he is frustrated. But when I think more carefully, I have to ask myself: how much of that is about his actual age and development, and how much comes from our deeply ingrained idea of what boys are supposed to be like?

I do not want him to grow into a man who has learned not to feel.

And I do not want the only sentence he knows how to say when he is hurt, afraid, or overwhelmed to be, “I’m fine.”

If a boy grows up constantly being told not to cry, not to be afraid, and not to appear weak, then why are we surprised when, as an adult, he struggles to explain what he feels, communicate in close relationships, understand someone else’s emotions, or deal with pressure in any way other than silence or anger?

As a woman, I may never fully understand what it feels like for a boy to grow into a man.

But I can at least try not to define too quickly what it means to “act like a boy.”

I can teach him that courage does not mean never feeling afraid, but being willing to face something even when fear is present. I can teach him that independence does not mean never needing anyone, but knowing when to ask for help and when to try on his own. I can teach him that responsibility does not mean suppressing emotion, but understanding that even when you are angry, there are still things you cannot do.

These lessons may sound as though they are only meant for him.

In many ways, they are also reminders for me.

Raising a child has a way of revealing beliefs in ourselves that we did not even realize were there. The way we understand men and women, strength and vulnerability, respect, responsibility, boundaries, and love all begin to appear in the ordinary rhythm of everyday parenting.

Sometimes I think I am simply teaching him.

Then I realize he is also forcing me to examine things I once believed without question.

I do not know how to raise what people might call an “excellent boy,” and I am less and less convinced that this is something a parent can design in advance anyway.

What I hope is that, as he grows, I can learn to understand him without rushing to shape him. I hope I can give him enough security while also teaching him that the world has boundaries. I want him to know that emotions are allowed, but that actions still carry responsibility.

Maybe one day he will no longer need me to help him name every feeling or remind him again and again what to do next. He will have his own friends, his own life, his own opinions, and perhaps one day he will become a man I cannot fully understand.

But before that happens, he is still a child.

And I am still a mother learning how to grow alongside this boy.
